The Problem

Everyday convenience creates a lasting waste challenge

Dining containers, drink bottles, utensils, and delivery packaging are used for minutes — and then become a waste problem that someone else has to handle.

35.7M tons

of plastic in U.S. municipal solid waste in 2018. Only 8.7% was recycled.

Source: U.S. EPA, 2018 data
What does this statistic measure?

These are EPA's most recent national figures for plastics in municipal solid waste — the trash and recycling from homes, schools, and businesses. They are historical (2018) numbers, not a measurement of all plastic production or of today's recycling rate. Plastic that wasn't recycled was mostly landfilled or burned for energy; it did not all enter the environment.

Harm to wildlife

NOAA reports that plastic bags, packing bands, six-pack rings, and other marine debris can entangle animals — restricting movement and feeding and potentially causing injury or death.

Why government has a role

Plastic pollution is a negative externality: its costs extend beyond a product's buyer and seller, falling on communities and ecosystems. Public policy can support research, shared infrastructure, and environmental protection that individual consumers or businesses may not fully finance.

So what does SMA want?

A combination of research support, practical demonstration projects, and public accountability for results — with a priority on preventing waste, since less disposal means less material to collect, process, or clean up.

Our Solutions

Better design. Practical reuse. Effective recovery.

We support technologies based on performance, not marketing claims. Each solution has to prove itself with measurable results.

Reusable dining systems

Durable containers circulate through distribution, convenient return stations, washing, and repeated use.

Measures of success

Return rates, completed reuse cycles, washing requirements, operating costs, and disposable containers avoided.

Improved material design

Packaging that uses less material or is designed for easier recovery after use — while staying affordable and practical.

Measures of success

Performance, durability, affordability, environmental effects, and compatibility with local collection and processing infrastructure.

Recycling improvements

Better sorting and processing technologies that recover useful materials from collected waste.

Measures of success

Material recovered, contamination levels, energy requirements, and cost — supported only when independent evidence shows improved recovery at reasonable cost.

How SMA evaluates a solution

Before endorsing a technology, we look at its whole life cycle: production → transportation → use → disposal. An alternative only earns our support if it produces an overall environmental benefit.

Example: "compostable" plastic

Commercially compostable plastic only breaks down in a commercial or industrial composting facility — not in a backyard bin or a landfill. EPA advises choosing it only where a local composting program actually accepts it. So SMA supports compostable packaging only where the right collection and processing exist.

Our Policy Agenda

Turning promising ideas into public policy

Three specific requests we bring to Congress.

1

Fund research

Congress authorizes and funds competitive grants for sustainable materials, reuse systems, and effective recycling technologies. Universities, public research institutions, and qualified innovators compete with defined goals.

2

Test solutions in communities

Demonstration grants help campuses and communities test projects under real conditions — like reusable dining containers paired with accessible return stations.

3

Require results

Funded projects report costs, participation, waste reduction, and environmental effects. Independent evaluation decides whether projects keep receiving support.

Who makes these decisions?

Congress

Authorizes programs and appropriates the funding — the target of our lobbying.

DOE

Department of Energy would administer technology-research grants.

EPA

Would administer waste-reduction demonstration grants.

Agency assignments are SMA's proposed framework, not an existing legislative package. Limited trials and performance requirements address concerns about public spending.

Take Action

Make sustainable innovation a policy priority

How membership and public support become political influence.

Contact Congress

Grassroots lobbying: supporters send personalized letters and emails, attend town halls, and join district-office meetings.

Meet with policymakers

Direct lobbying: SMA requests congressional-office meetings and presents a concise policy brief with published evidence and campus findings.

Build a coalition

We seek prospective partners: university researchers, environmental organizations, campus sustainability offices, and credible sustainable-technology businesses.

Sample advocacy letter

Copy it, personalize it, and send it to your representative and senators.

Dear [Representative / Senator Name], I am a constituent from [City, State] and a supporter of the Sustainable Materials Alliance. I am writing about plastic waste. In 2018, the United States generated about 35.7 million tons of plastic in municipal solid waste, and only 8.7% was recycled. Please support competitive funding for sustainable-materials research and campus waste-reduction demonstrations, with independent evaluation and public reporting of results. Programs like these would let universities and communities test practical solutions — such as reusable dining-container systems — and would continue funding only the projects that show measurable results. Thank you for your time and consideration. Sincerely, [Your Name] [Your Address]
